# Complete Literature-Based Piano Chord Voicing Catalogue

> **Scope:** a literature-oriented catalogue of chord voicings and piano-voicing systems that are documented in pedagogical, arranging, jazz, classical, contemporary, and related music literature, restricted to voicings that can be physically realized on a piano.
>
> **Important:** this is **not** a mathematical list of every possible arrangement of notes on 88 keys. The number of physical realizations is enormous. Instead, this catalogue records **documented voicing concepts and formulas**, then gives representative piano realizations. Different authors may use different names for similar structures; terminology is preserved rather than forced into one universal system.

# 1. Scope and terminology

A **chord** describes harmonic pitch content; a **voicing** describes how that content is distributed in register and among voices.

The same chord can therefore have many voicings.

For example, Cmaj7 may appear as:

- C–E–G–B
- E–G–B–C
- C–G–B–E
- E–B–D–G (a Cmaj9 rootless structure)
- C–G–D–E–B
- C2–G2–E3–B3
- C3–E3–B3–D4–G4

These are not all the same *voicing concept*, even when they share the same pitch-class content.

## Catalogue principle

This document distinguishes:

1. **Chord quality** — e.g. Cmaj7
2. **Voicing formula** — e.g. 1–3–5–7
3. **Register** — e.g. C2–E2–G2–B2
4. **Voicing system** — e.g. drop-2, shell, rootless, quartal
5. **Function/context** — tonic, dominant, ii–V–I, modal, etc.
6. **Source/tradition** — where the terminology or technique is documented

## Piano requirement

A voicing is included when it can be simultaneously realized by one pianist on a normal piano, either:

- in one hand,
- between both hands,
- or with bass/root supplied by one hand.

A structure that is theoretically meaningful but cannot reasonably be played simultaneously on piano is not treated as a piano voicing.

# 2. Basic triadic voicings

## Major triad

Formula:

`1–3–5`

C example:

`C–E–G`

Common realizations:

- Root position: C–E–G
- First inversion: E–G–C
- Second inversion: G–C–E
- Open: C–G–E
- Spread: C–G–E (different octaves)
- Octave-doubled: C–G–C–E
- Root-doubled: C–E–G–C
- Fifth-doubled: C–G–E–G
- Two-octave spread: C2–G2–E3

## Minor triad

Formula:

`1–♭3–5`

C:

`C–E♭–G`

Same principal inversion, open, spread, and doubling systems as major.

## Diminished triad

Formula:

`1–♭3–♭5`

C:

`C–E♭–G♭`

## Augmented triad

Formula:

`1–3–♯5`

C:

`C–E–G♯`

## Suspended triads

### Sus2

`1–2–5`

C:

`C–D–G`

### Sus4

`1–4–5`

C:

`C–F–G`

### Additive triads

Documented constructions include:

- add2
- add4
- add6
- add9
- add♯11
- add♭6
- add♭9

These may be voiced in close, open, spread, or cluster-like arrangements.

# 4. Closed and open position

## Closed position

All chord tones occupy the smallest practical register.

Cmaj7:

`C–E–G–B`

## Open position

One or more voices are displaced by an octave.

Examples:

`C–G–E–B`

`C–E–B–G`

`C–G–B–E`

## Spread voicing

A broad distribution of voices, frequently with a larger interval in the bass and closer intervals above.

Example:

`C2–G2–E3–B3`

The exact meaning of "open" and "spread" varies between authors and traditions.

# 5. Doubling and spacing

Classical and arranging literature frequently treats doubling as an independent voicing parameter.

Possible doubled tones include:

- root
- third
- fifth
- seventh
- ninth
- melody
- bass octave

Common piano structures:

`C–C–E–G`

`C–G–C–E`

`C–G–E–G`

`C–E–G–C–E`

Doubling can also be used to reinforce melody or bass.

# 7. Shell voicings

A **shell voicing** reduces the chord to its most characteristic tones.

Berklee's piano-voicing material emphasizes the chord's basic identifying tones and specifically discusses the 3rd and 7th for seventh chords. [1]

## Basic dominant shell

`1–3–♭7`

C7:

`C–E–B♭`

## Alternative shell

`1–♭7–3`

C7:

`C–B♭–E`

## Rootless shell

`3–♭7`

C7:

`E–B♭`

These are especially useful for:

- comping
- voice leading
- bass-player situations
- II–V–I progressions

`1–3–♭7`

`3–♭7–9`

### Minor

`1–♭3–5`

`1–3–7`

# 9. Rootless voicings

Rootless voicings omit the root because the bass player or harmonic context supplies it.

`E–G–B–D`

### Major 7 with 9 and 6/13

`3–5–7–9–13`

Cmaj13:

`E–G–B–D–A`

### Minor 7

`♭3–5–♭7–9`

Cm9:

`E♭–G–B♭–D`

### Dominant 7

`3–♭7–9–13`

C13:

`E–B♭–D–A`

Common ingredients:

`3–♭7–♭9–♯9`

`3–♭7–♯9–♭13`

`3–♭7–♭5–♭9`

Rootless voicings are extensively treated in jazz-piano literature, including Levine's treatment of left-hand voicings. [2]

# 10. Left-hand voicings

# 11. Right-hand / melody voicings

A common piano practice is to treat the melody as the highest chord tone.

Examples:

`LH: C–G`

`RH: E–B–D`

or

`LH: C`

`RH: G–B–D–E`

The melody may be:

- root
- third
- fifth
- seventh
- ninth
- eleventh
- thirteenth
- altered extension

The voicing is therefore often defined relative to the melody rather than only by root position.

# 12. Drop voicings

Drop voicings originate in close-position four-part writing and became fundamental to arranging and jazz chord voicing.

## Drop-2

Take the second-highest voice of a close-position chord and lower it one octave.

For Cmaj7:

Close:

`C–E–G–B`

Drop-2:

`G–C–E–B`

Other inversions are possible.

## Drop-3

Take the third-highest voice and lower it one octave.

## Drop-2-and-4

Lower the second- and fourth-highest voices by an octave.

## Drop-2-and-3

Lower the second- and third-highest voices.

## Other compound drops

Literature and arranging practice also permit combinations of displaced voices. Such labels should be used carefully because terminology varies by source.

# 17. Quartal / fourth voicings

Quartal harmony stacks fourths rather than thirds.

Examples:

`C–F–B♭`

`C–F–B♭–E♭`

`D–G–C–F`

These structures may be:

- purely quartal
- mixed quartal/tertian
- used as modal voicings
- used as dominant structures
- used as upper structures

Mark Levine explicitly includes a chapter/section on "Fourth Chords." [2]

---

# 18. Quintal voicings

Quintal voicings stack fifths:

`C–G–D`

`C–G–D–A`

`C–G–D–A–E`

Because a fifth and its inversion are a fourth, quintal and quartal constructions are closely related, but the registral direction and conceptual emphasis can differ.

# 19. Upper-structure triads

An upper-structure voicing combines:

1. a bass/root or shell
2. a triad above it

Example:

C7♯9♭13 can be represented using an E♭ major triad over C:

`C + E♭–G–B♭`

or other upper structures depending on the intended tensions.

Common upper-structure practice includes triads that produce:

- ♭9
- ♯9
- ♯11
- ♭13
- combinations of altered tensions

Levine's *The Jazz Piano Book* explicitly contains a section on upper structures. [2]

# 20. Polychords and slash structures

A polychord places one chord over another.

Examples:

### D/C

`C–D–F♯–A`

### E♭/C

`C–E♭–G–B♭`

### D♭/C

`C–D♭–F–A♭`

These can create:

- altered dominants
- extended major chords
- modal colors
- bitonal sonorities

Slash notation may alternatively indicate a bass note rather than a true polychord.

# 22. So What / modal voicings

Modal jazz introduced highly influential fourth-based structures.

A canonical type uses:

- stacked fourths
- a characteristic added third or sixth
- broad spacing
- modal rather than functional interpretation

Example generic structure:

`C–F–B♭–E♭–A`

These voicings are associated strongly with the modal-jazz sound and are discussed in Levine's treatment of "So What" chords and fourth chords. [2]

# 23. Block chords

Block-chord practice harmonizes a melody note with chord tones.

Common systems include:

- close-position block chords
- locked-hands style
- four-way close
- drop-2 block chords
- octave melody + inner chord
- diminished passing-chord techniques
- parallel block structures

The exact terminology varies substantially among jazz, arranging, and pedagogical sources.

# 30. Voice-leading systems

A voicing is often defined by its movement between chords rather than by a single isolated chord.

Important systems include:

## Common-tone voice leading

Retain shared chord tones.

## Guide-tone voice leading

Emphasize:

- 3rd
- 7th

especially through ii–V–I progressions.

## Contrary motion

Bass and upper voices move in opposite directions.

## Chromatic voice leading

Move individual chord tones by semitone.

## Stepwise upper-voice motion

Keep the bass stable while upper voices move by scale step.

## Constant-structure harmony

Move the same voicing shape chromatically or diatonically.

## Planing

Move a chord structure in parallel.

# 31. Piano-playability rules

For this catalogue, "piano playable" means the simultaneous notes can be physically produced by a pianist.

We should distinguish:

### Easily playable

Normal hand span and comfortable register.

### Playable

Requires ordinary two-hand redistribution or moderate stretch.

### Advanced playable

Requires large span, octave displacement, or specialized technique.

### Extreme playable

Possible on the 88-key instrument but physically demanding.

### Not a piano voicing

Requires more simultaneous notes than can reasonably be performed by one pianist or depends on physically impossible spacing.

This distinction matters because a mathematically valid pitch collection is not automatically a practical piano voicing.

# Appendix B — Important terminology warning

There is **no universally standardized dictionary of piano voicing names**.

For example:

- "open voicing"
- "spread voicing"
- "rootless voicing"
- "shell voicing"
- "upper structure"
- "drop-2"
- "quartal voicing"

may have overlapping meanings in different traditions.

Therefore, a serious future expansion of this catalogue should record:

**name + exact note formula + source + context**

rather than relying on the name alone.

---

# Appendix C — What this catalogue deliberately does NOT claim

This document does **not** claim that:

- every possible piano note combination has been enumerated;
- every voicing term has one universal definition;
- every historical voicing can be attributed to a single person;
- every structure in a mathematical chord database is a documented musical practice;
- a voicing's existence in one book means it is standard across all traditions.

The goal is instead a **literature-grounded, piano-playable taxonomy** that can be expanded as additional sources are researched.
